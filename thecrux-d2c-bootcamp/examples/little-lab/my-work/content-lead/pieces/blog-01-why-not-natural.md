# Blog 01 — Why we never use the word "natural"

**Channel**: Blog (long-form, ~1,100 words)
**Calendar slot**: Day 5, May 17 Sat
**Theme served**: Anti-positioning wedge
**Source**: CLAUDE.md Section 3 (anti-positioning) + Market Analyst pattern across the set

---

There is a word that does more selling in our category than anything else, and we have decided we will not use it.

The word is "natural".

If you walk into any pharmacy in Mumbai or open Amazon's baby skincare aisle, the word is on most bottles. It is in the headline, in the product description, in the founder's video. It is the most-used adjective in the category by a wide margin. We have not used it once in three years of building Little Lab, and we will not start.

Here is why.

**"Natural" is not a regulated word in India.**

The Drugs and Cosmetics Act does not define it. The Bureau of Indian Standards has no test for it. The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) does not check it. Which means any brand can put "natural" on any bottle as long as the rest of the claims pass. A brand selling a baby lotion with 0.5% calendula extract and 99.5% paraffin oil can legally put "natural" on the front. Many do.

You, the parent, then read "natural" and form an impression: ingredients that are simple, recognisable, plant-based, safe. None of that is in the legal definition. None of that is what the word actually promises. It is a Rorschach test with a Rs. 549 price tag.

**The harder problem is what "natural" replaces.**

Replacing what, you ask? Replacing the actual ingredient list.

Take a competitor's bottle off the shelf at any Apollo Pharmacy. Read the back. Most of the time you will find a list that looks something like this:

> Aqua, plant-derived extracts, natural oils, ayurvedic blend, vitamin E, preservatives (food-grade), fragrance (nature-identical).

Seven items. None of which is specific enough to look up. "Plant-derived extracts" could be anything from chamomile to bamboo. "Ayurvedic blend" is not an ingredient; it is a marketing category. "Preservatives (food-grade)" tells you nothing about whether it is potassium sorbate or sodium benzoate, both of which are food-grade but behave very differently on a newborn's skin barrier.

Now flip our bottle.

> Aqua, glycerin (3%), caprylic/capric triglyceride, cetearyl alcohol, calendula officinalis extract (1.5%), tocopherol (0.3%), xanthan gum, citric acid, sodium benzoate (0.4%), potassium sorbate (0.2%), allantoin (0.5%).

Eleven items. Each one is a thing you can look up. Five of them carry percentages because the regulator allows us to publish those percentages and we choose to. The two preservatives are named, not described.

That list is not prettier than "natural". It is uglier. "Cetearyl alcohol" sounds, on first reading, like something you would not want on a baby. (It is not. It is a fatty alcohol, the same kind your skin makes naturally. It is in almost every moisturiser.) But you can look it up. You can ask your paediatrician. You can decide.

**"Natural" closes the conversation. The full list opens it.**

That is the bet we have made. We are betting that the parent we want, the one who reads the back of the bottle in the aisle at Apollo Pharmacy, would rather see an honest list of eleven things she can look up than a friendly list of seven things she cannot. We are betting that this parent exists in enough numbers to build a brand on. So far, the data says she does. Of the 87 customer messages we read this past month, 14 of them said some version of "I switched to you because I could actually read the ingredient list".

**The cost of this choice.**

There is a cost. "Natural" is a head-term that drives organic search in our category. We rank below our competitors on it because we will not optimise for it. We will likely never rank for it. Our paid ad CTR is lower on the keywords that include "natural" than on the keywords that include "ingredient list", and the keyword pool for "ingredient list" is much smaller than the pool for "natural". This means our addressable market through search is genuinely narrower than the brands that do use the word.

We have made peace with that. The parent who searches "natural baby lotion" and clicks on the cheapest result is not our parent. She is buying on a different promise. If she ever flips a bottle and reads the back, she might become our parent, but it will not be the search that converts her.

**What we use instead.**

You will not see "natural" on a Little Lab bottle, in a Little Lab ad, in a Little Lab email, in a Little Lab Instagram caption. You will see, instead, things like:

- "Eleven ingredients. The full list."
- "Paediatrically reviewed for newborns."
- "1% piroctone olamine. Named, dosed, on the label."
- "Small batch."
- "For the parent who reads the back of the bottle."

These are all things that mean something specific. None of them is a Rorschach test. All of them are things you can verify by looking at the product or by asking a paediatrician.

This is the harder route. We picked it on purpose. If you have read this far, you already know why.
