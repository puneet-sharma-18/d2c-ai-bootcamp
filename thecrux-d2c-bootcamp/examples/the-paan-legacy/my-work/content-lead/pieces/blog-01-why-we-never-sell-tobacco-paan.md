# Blog 01 — Why we never sell tobacco paan (and what we lost by saying no)

**Channel**: Blog (long-form, ~900 words)
**Calendar slot**: Day 3, May 16 Fri
**Theme served**: Anti-positioning wedge
**Source**: CLAUDE.md Section 3 (anti-positioning) + Market Analyst (no competitor leads on this)

---

The first decision we made when we started The Paan Legacy was not a recipe. It was a "no".

The "no" was tobacco.

If you have ever stepped into a paan-wala shop in Mumbai or Delhi or Hyderabad, you know the layout. There is the meetha tin, the saadha tin, the masala tin, the tobacco tin. The tobacco tin is where the margin is. It is where most of the daily revenue comes from. Tobacco paan is what keeps that shop running. The sweet paan is what keeps it traditional.

When we built our first product list, we decided we would not have a tobacco tin.

This is not a small decision. The paan category in India is roughly a ₹20,000 crore market (we cannot give you a citation that is more precise than that; the category is too informal to measure cleanly). Tobacco-laced paan is somewhere between 60% and 75% of that. By choosing not to sell tobacco paan, we walked away from the majority of the category's revenue on day one.

Here is what we lost by saying no.

**We lost the addiction-driven repeat purchase.** A tobacco-paan customer comes back every day. Often twice. The unit economics of that customer are unbeatable. Our customer comes back every six weeks. We make six weeks of unit economics work; the paan-wala makes one day of it work twelve times.

**We lost easy distribution.** Half the wholesalers in this category will not stock a brand without a tobacco SKU. They are not being unreasonable; they are reading their own customer demand. When we approached our first three wholesalers in Mumbai, two said no. The third said yes only after our Diwali corporate gifting numbers came in.

**We lost the price-sensitive market.** A loose tobacco paan is ₹15 to ₹25. Our cheapest paan is ₹179. We are 10x more expensive than the default. We cannot win the customer who is choosing between ₹15 and ₹17 today. We can only win the customer who has stopped going to the paan-wala for reasons not about price.

Here is what we gained.

**We gained the woman of the house as our buyer.** In most Indian homes, the man buys the paan-wala paan. The woman has opinions about it but does not buy it. Our customer is the woman. She is buying for the Sunday lunch ritual, for her parents, for the corporate Diwali list. The buyer decision-maker is different. So is the product.

**We gained the gifting occasion.** Tobacco paan is not giftable. You cannot send tobacco to your office HR's Diwali list. You cannot put it in a wedding favour box. We can. We did. Our corporate gifting line, even at our small scale, did 18% of our revenue in October-November 2025.

**We gained the next generation.** The 28-to-44 person who has watched a parent or an uncle die from tongue cancer does not buy tobacco paan. They might buy a sweet paan once or twice a year for nostalgia. We sell them that paan. We sell it to them every six weeks because we make a product their kids can also have a small bite of, and because the brand on the box is not a tobacco brand.

The math worked out. Eventually.

It took us 14 months to break even on our first product line. Most paan businesses break even faster because tobacco subsidises the meetha. We have nothing to subsidise the meetha. The meetha has to pay for itself.

We do not regret the decision. If anything, the decision is the brand. We do not sell tobacco paan. We never will. We do not sell "spit-out" paan either, because every spit-out paan is a vehicle for an ingredient (mostly tobacco) that we would not put in our own children's gift box. Eat, do not spit.

If you are a customer reading this, the takeaway is simple: every paan we send you is meant to be eaten, swallowed, enjoyed and forgotten until the next one. There is no tin in our shop where tobacco lives.

If you are a wholesaler reading this, the takeaway is that we will not change our mind. We will, however, prove the numbers. If the numbers are there, the wholesale relationships follow. We are slower; we are not less serious.

If you are another founder thinking about a category like ours, the question we found ourselves asking again and again was: what is the "no" you are willing to make that the category cannot follow? Most of our competitive position comes from the no, not the yes.
