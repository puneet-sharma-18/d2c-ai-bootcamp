## Title
How my grandmother made paan on Sundays. The recipe I tried to reconstruct for 20 years.

## Subtitle
The almond, the rose, the gulkand, the saffron. No tobacco. No spitting. A 90-minute Sunday afternoon in a Lucknow kitchen that I have been trying to put in a box ever since.

---

My grandmother's name was Saroj. She made paan on Sundays.

Lunch in our house was a long thing. Two dals, rice, four sabzis, a meat for the men, raita, achaar. The eating took an hour. The talking took another. The paan came at the end of the second hour, when she would go to the kitchen and come back with a small steel tray and start wrapping.

She made one paan at a time. She did not make a batch. She wrapped, handed it to whoever was nearest, and started the next. By the time she had made twelve, the room was quiet. Everyone was chewing.

There was no tobacco in her paan. There was no supari either, not because she had a position on it, but because she did not like the noise it made between teeth. There was no zarda. The paan she made was a digestive. Almond, rose, gulkand, fennel, cardamom, occasionally a single saffron thread on the days she was feeling celebratory. We ate the whole thing. Swallowed it. Asked for water.

She died in 2003. I was 19.

For the next 20 years I tried to find that paan.

I tried Banarasi paan shops. I tried Calcutta meetha paan. I tried the Lucknow stalls I remembered from my school holidays. None of them was hers. Most of them were tobacco vehicles. The ones that were not used a sweet base that was too aggressive, a chashni instead of a gulkand, a syrupy paste that made the leaf damp within ten seconds. I tried to make it at home. I bought betel leaves at Crawford Market. I bought gulkand from three different shops in Chembur. I roasted my own fennel. None of it was hers. The leaf was too thick. The gulkand was too cooked. The almonds were not slivered the way she slivered them.

I gave up around 2018.

Then in 2021 I was at a friend's house in Pune and someone served me a Bombay Sweet Shop paan after dinner. It was good. It was beautifully plated. It was also not what I was looking for. It was a chef's paan. Saroj's paan was a grandmother's paan. There is a difference.

That was the night I decided I would make this thing myself, not as a hobby, but as a product. Because if I had spent 20 years looking for it, somebody else had too. And the answer would not come from a chef. It would come from sitting in a kitchen and getting the slivering wrong for six months until the slivering was right.

We launched The Paan Legacy in 2023.

The Almond Delight is the closest I have got to Saroj's paan. The almonds are slivered, not chopped. The rose petals are dried, not freeze-dried. The gulkand is slow-cooked. The saffron is one thread, Kashmiri. The fennel is hand-roasted in our kitchen in small batches. The cardamom is green, pod-cracked the day of. The betel leaf is sourced fresh, not stored, never refrigerated more than 36 hours.

There is no tobacco. There is no supari. There is no zarda. There is no spitting.

I get reviews now where people say "this tastes like my grandmother's paan". The first time I read one of those reviews, I cried for about ten minutes. I had spent 20 years failing to make this paan, and a stranger in Lucknow had eaten one of our boxes and written that their father said it reminded him of a shop in Hazratganj that has not existed for 30 years.

That is the entire reason this brand exists.

We are not building a premium snack. We are not building a digestif. We are rebuilding a Sunday afternoon that the category gave up on around the time the tobacco habit took over. The paan was a digestive for 2,000 years before it became a stain on a wall in the last 100. The first 2,000 years are the part we want.

If your grandmother also made paan on Sundays, and if you also stopped eating it because the loose paan-wala version was never the same, we made this for you. The Almond Delight is ₹299 for a box of 6. Allergen note: contains tree nuts. Tobacco-free. FSSAI registered.

You will know in the first bite whether your grandmother would have liked it. If she would have, please write to me and tell me her name. I read those emails. I keep a list.

Puneet
